\# 2. Introduction to the Domain



\## 2.1 Domain Selected



The domain selected for this project is Hostel Management and within it the sub-domain of mess management in a residential technical institution.



\## 2.2 Background of the Domain



Residential institutions house large student populations who depend on a central mess for their daily meals. At Thapar Institute of Engineering and Technology, Patiala, every hostel runs its own mess. The hostel studied here is Hostel D (Neeram Hall) serves close to 800 residents across three meals a day.



The cooking and serving is contracted out to a third-party catering provider. Oversight remains with the institution like the hostel warden holds administrative authority and an elected student mess committee represents student interests and monitors food quality. Between these three parties, mess management comes down to four recurring activities such as deciding and announcing the menu, serving meals, hearing what students think of the food and settling rebates for students who are away.



\## 2.3 Existing Practices



The mess at Hostel D is not run on paper alone. Parts of it have already moved online but they have done so separately and that is where the difficulty begins. Three functions are handled through three unrelated channels.



The menu reaches students over hostel WhatsApp groups. This works well enough on the day it is sent but the message soon disappears under later group activity. If the menu changes the revision looks no different from the original and nobody can later establish what was actually served on a given date.



Rebates are claimed on a printed form submitted to the warden's office. Once the form is handed in the student hears nothing further. There is no acknowledgement or no way to check whether the request has been approved and no statement of how many rebate days were finally credited.



Feedback and complaints go through a QR code displayed in the mess which opens a web form. Students can submit their views but the process ends there. No confirmation comes back and no one sees what action followed and the collected responses are never summarised or shared with the students who wrote them.



Each of these channels stores its data separately and none of them can read the others. A complaint about a bad meal cannot be tied to the menu served that day. Ratings cannot be compared across weeks to see whether anything improved. Rebate records sit apart from any record of who actually ate. The information exists but it exists in pieces.



\## 2.4 Importance and Relevance of Automation



When roughly 800 students depend on one facility, small inefficiencies add up quickly — into paperwork for the warden's office, into repeated complaints about the same dish and into a general sense among students that raising an issue changes nothing.



Bringing these functions into one system addresses this in several ways. It gives students a single place to look with a menu that stays available instead of scrolling past. It makes requests traceable so that a rebate application or a complaint carries a visible status and cannot quietly disappear. It allows decisions to rest on data like if a dish is rated poorly for three weeks running then the committee can show that to the caterer rather than argue from memory. It fixes responsibility since role-based access means only the warden can approve a rebate and every approval is recorded against a name and it removes the manual counting of rebate days from the fee adjustment process.



The point of automation here is not to replace paperwork. Much of this already happens electronically. What is missing is connection that is a single system in which what one function records and the others can use. That is what makes this sub-domain worth building software for.
